Topic

Alcohol

Europe has among the highest rates of alcohol-related deaths in the world (WHO). Here is how much harm it does, who drinks most heavily, and what it means for a country.

01

What this means for a country

Alcohol and mental health are bound together: heavy drinking deepens depression and anxiety, and people who are struggling often drink more.

Longer lives

Alcohol causes liver disease, several cancers, heart disease, injuries and road deaths, and it is closely linked to suicide (WHO). Young adults are hit hardest, so less heavy drinking means many more years of life.

A stronger economy

The cost falls on hospitals, police and courts, but the largest part is lost work: absence, accidents, lower productivity and people who die or stop working early.

Safer families and streets

Alcohol harms people other than the drinker: through violence, neglect, drink-driving and the strain on partners and children.

04

What helps

The measures with the strongest evidence, as summarised in the World Health Organization's SAFER package.

Price

Alcohol taxes and minimum prices reduce heavy drinking most, because the heaviest drinkers buy the cheapest alcohol.

Availability and marketing

Limits on where, when and to whom alcohol is sold, and on advertising and sponsorship, lower how much people drink.

Drink-driving

Low blood-alcohol limits with random breath testing prevent road deaths and injuries.

Early help and treatment

A short conversation with a doctor or nurse about drinking helps many people cut down. Treatment for alcohol dependence works, and works better when depression or anxiety is treated at the same time.

05

About these figures

The death rate counts only deaths certified as caused by an alcohol-use disorder (ICD-10 F10). Most deaths caused by alcohol, such as liver disease, cancers and injuries, are recorded under other causes, and countries certify deaths differently, so the rate is a narrow measure and differences between countries are partly about recording.

Heavy drinking means more than 60 g of pure alcohol on one occasion, about six drinks of 10 g each. The survey runs about every five years; the latest comparable figures are from 2019. Switzerland does not take part, the latest figures for the United Kingdom (and for Finland's drinking habits) are from 2014, and countries outside Europe are not shown here yet.

Figures are shown as published by Eurostat and are not part of any index score. Methodology · Depression
