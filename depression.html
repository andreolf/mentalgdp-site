Topic

Depression

The most common mental illness, and one of the biggest reasons people stop working or living fully. Here is how common it is across Europe, who it affects most, and what it means for a country.

01

What this means for a country

Depression is more than feeling low. It drains energy, sleep, concentration and hope for weeks at a time, and it is treatable.

Longer, healthier lives

People with depression and other mental disorders die earlier, on average, than the rest of the population: from suicide, but also from heart disease and other illness that is found and treated later (Walker et al., 2015; Plana-Ripoll et al., 2019). Less depression means more years of life, and better ones.

A stronger economy

Depression is a leading cause of disability worldwide (WHO). Most of its cost is not treatment but lost work: sick days, people at work who cannot function well, and early exits from the labour market.

Families and the next generation

Depression affects the people around the person who has it: partners, carers and children. Treating it early protects whole households, not only individuals.

04

What helps

What countries can do, in broad strokes. The index will track some of these as care indicators.

Treatment that people can reach

Talking therapies and medication both work for many people, and work best when started early. Many people with depression never receive treatment, because of waiting lists, cost, stigma or not knowing where to go.

Spotting it early

Family doctors, schools and workplaces are where depression is most often first noticed. Short screening questions, like the ones used in this survey, help people get to care sooner.

The conditions around people

Loneliness, debt, insecure work and poor housing all raise the risk of depression. The index's conditions pillar tracks some of these, such as social support and life satisfaction.

Workplaces

Phased returns to work, adjusted duties and managers trained to talk about mental health keep people in work and shorten time off.

05

About these figures

Depressive symptoms are measured with the PHQ-8, eight questions about the past two weeks: low mood, loss of interest, sleep, tiredness, appetite, self-worth, concentration, and feeling slowed down or restless. It is a screening tool, not a diagnosis.

The survey runs about every five years, so the latest comparable figures are from 2019, before the pandemic. Switzerland does not take part, the latest figures for the United Kingdom are from 2014, and countries outside Europe use different surveys and are not shown here yet.
